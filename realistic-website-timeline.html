How Long Should a Website Take to Build? A Realistic Timeline

Timeline questions come up right alongside cost questions, and the honest answer follows a similar pattern - it depends on real, specific factors, but there are genuine industry-standard ranges worth knowing before you get either a suspiciously fast or suspiciously slow quote.

Landing pages and single-page sites: 3-5 days

A focused, single-page site with a clear scope (one design direction, provided content, standard contact form) typically takes 3-5 working days from kickoff to launch. This assumes content and any brand assets are ready before development starts - waiting on copy or images is the single biggest cause of delay at this scale.

Business websites (5-8 pages): 5-7 days

Multi-page business sites take longer mainly because of the sheer number of pages needing content placement, plus typically more back-and-forth on design direction across a full site rather than one page. Basic SEO setup (meta tags, structured data) usually happens in this window too.

E-commerce stores: 5-10 days

The range here is wide because "e-commerce store" varies hugely in scope - a WooCommerce store with 20 straightforward products is much faster than one needing custom variant logic, courier integration, and a custom checkout flow. Payment gateway integration and testing alone can add several days if it's your first time setting one up for that specific market.

Custom web applications: 10-14+ days

Anything with genuine custom logic - user authentication, a database-backed dashboard, unique business rules - scales in timeline with feature count more than with any fixed formula. A genuinely complex system (like a full school management platform) can run into weeks or months, not because of inefficiency but because there's simply more distinct, interconnected functionality to build and test properly.

What actually causes projects to run over timeline

  • Content not being ready - by far the most common cause; development often has to pause waiting on copy, photos, or product data
  • Scope creep - "can we also add..." requests mid-project, each reasonable individually but adding up to meaningfully more work than originally scoped
  • Slow feedback cycles - if revision rounds take a week to get reviewed and approved, that adds directly to calendar time even if actual development time hasn't changed
  • Third-party integration surprises - a payment gateway or courier API behaving differently than documented is a real, common source of unplanned delay
  • Underestimated original scope - sometimes a project genuinely needed more time than the initial quote assumed, which is why a written scope document matters (see our brief-writing guide)

How to keep your project on the fast end of these ranges

  1. Have your content (copy, images, product data) ready before development starts, not partway through
  2. Turn around revision feedback quickly - even a 24-48 hour review cycle keeps momentum
  3. Resist adding new scope mid-project; note it for a phase two instead
  4. Front-load any third-party integrations for early testing, rather than leaving them for the final days

Should you be suspicious of a very fast quoted timeline?

A full custom e-commerce store quoted at "2 days" for a first-time client relationship is usually either underscoped (missing real requirements) or means corners will be cut on testing. A realistic timeline that matches the ranges above, with room built in for revisions, is a better sign of a developer who's actually thought through the work than an aggressively fast promise.
